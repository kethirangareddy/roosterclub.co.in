Gamefowl colour genetics, simplified

Rooster Club knowledge series · ~9 min read

Why does pairing two Peela birds sometimes give you a Kaki chick? Why does a colour seem to "skip" a generation and reappear? Colour in gamefowl can feel like magic, but it follows rules — and once you grasp a few basics, you can predict outcomes far better and describe your birds more accurately. This guide keeps the science plain and practical for Indian breeders working with named Aseel colour types such as Peela, Kaki, Dega, Nemali and Reza.

The one idea behind everything: genes come in pairs

Every bird carries two copies of each colour gene — one from the sire, one from the dam. When it breeds, it passes just one of those two copies to each chick, at random. The chick's colour comes from whatever combination of copies it happens to inherit from both parents. That single fact — two copies in, one copy passed on — is the whole foundation of colour prediction.

Dominant versus recessive

Some colour genes are dominant: a bird only needs one copy for that colour to show. Others are recessive: the colour appears only when the bird inherits two copies, one from each parent. This is why a recessive colour can vanish for a generation and then return. A bird carrying one copy of a recessive colour looks like its dominant colour but secretly "carries" the hidden one — breeders call this being a carrier.

  • Dominant colour: shows with one copy; harder to "hide" but also harder to lose.
  • Recessive colour: shows only with two copies; can be carried invisibly and reappear later.
  • Carrier: a bird that looks one colour but passes on a hidden one to some of its chicks.

How this plays out with named colours

The exact genetics behind each Aseel colour type involve several interacting genes, and local naming traditions vary by region, so treat what follows as a working model rather than a laboratory result. The practical point is the pattern: if a colour behaves recessively in your line, two visible parents of that colour will breed true most of the time, while pairing a visible bird to a carrier will give you a mix. If a colour behaves dominantly, you can get it from a single parent that shows it, even when the other parent is a different colour.

The honest way to learn your own birds is to test-mate and record. Pair known colours, write down what every chick turns out to be, and over a couple of seasons you will see which colours in your stock are dominant, which are recessive, and which lines carry hidden colours.

You do not need a genetics degree to breed for colour — you need a notebook, a few planned pairings, and the patience to watch what comes out of the nest.

Predicting chick colour, step by step

Here is a simple way to reason about a pairing before you make it:

  • Write down each parent's colour and, if you know it, what colours it has thrown before.
  • Decide whether the colour you want behaves dominantly or recessively in your line.
  • For a recessive colour, both parents must at least carry it for any chicks to show it.
  • For a dominant colour, one showing parent is enough for some chicks to show it.
  • Expect a range in each clutch — chance decides which copies each chick gets, so ratios only even out over many chicks.

Beyond colour: don't breed for paint alone

Colour is the easiest trait to see and the most tempting to chase, but it is only skin deep. A perfectly coloured bird with poor structure, weak constitution or bad temperament is a poor breeder. Use colour as one criterion among many — health, conformation, vigour and temperament come first, and colour is the finishing touch on an already sound bird.

Describing colour honestly when you sell

Because colour names vary across regions, clear description matters at sale time. State the colour type as you know it, add good natural-light photos, and if you know the bird's parents' colours, say so — a knowledgeable buyer values that lineage detail.
